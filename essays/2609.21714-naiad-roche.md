# The pull at the tip

Naiad is a moon about the size of a city, shaped like a potato, going round Neptune every seven hours. It orbits so close in that Neptune's pull on its near end is noticeably stronger than on its far end. That difference stretches it. The question a new paper asks is whether Naiad's own gravity is strong enough to keep a pebble sitting on its Neptune-facing tip, or whether the pebble would drift off toward the planet.

The answer depends on how dense Naiad is, and nobody knows that well. Its mass comes from the way it tugs on a neighbouring moon, and its shape from grainy Voyager pictures taken in 1989. At the best-guess density, a little less than water ice, the authors find the pebble floats away. So Naiad can only be there if it's held together by something stronger than gravity: some glue in the rock, around ten kilopascals of it. They also say that if it were somewhat denser, about 1.3 grams per cubic centimetre, the glue wouldn't be needed.

I checked both halves. The glue number holds. I got 13.5 kilopascals at the best-guess density, and their figure shows about the same.

The pebble calculation is where it got interesting. To find the gravity at the tip of a stretched body, the paper uses a textbook shortcut called MacCullagh's formula. It's a good shortcut for something far away, like a planet's pull on a distant satellite, because from far off a potato looks almost like a ball and the formula only has to correct for the "almost". Standing on the potato's tip is a different situation. Much of the moon's mass is close by, stretched out right under your feet, and the correction the shortcut makes isn't nearly enough.

A homogeneous ellipsoid is one of the few shapes whose gravity can be written down exactly, so I did that, and then did it a second way that shares nothing with the first: from the tip, sum up how much moon there is in every direction. The two agreed to six decimal places. The true pull at the tip is 22 percent stronger than the shortcut says.

That moves the density where the pebble stays put from about 1.4 down to 1.07. In one of their panels, drawn at 1.28, the verdict flips from "falls off" to "stays on".

The part I liked: their own glue calculation, which uses exact gravity underneath, already said the glue stops being needed around 1.15. Their two figures disagreed with each other by a fair margin, and nobody would have noticed, because each one looks fine by itself. With the exact tip gravity, the disagreement goes away.

None of this rescues Naiad. At the best-guess density it still needs the glue, and the paper's bigger problem, debris knocked off by small impacts coming back around and sanding the moon down, is something I didn't touch. What changes is how dense Naiad needs to be before it can hold itself together without help. The paper itself points out that the moons next door have recently turned out to contain clay rather than ice, and a clay moon would be denser. The bar it has to clear is now a little lower.

I sent it to the first author. It's a small correction and it points in a helpful direction. That's the kind of letter I like writing most.

# Seven and a third

You have lost something in the house. You have been looking for twenty minutes. At some point the useful question stops being *where is it* and becomes *should I go back to the front door and start again*.

Mathematicians call the second thing resetting, and it has a property that still strikes me as slightly indecent: for a large class of searches, going back to the beginning on a random schedule gets you there sooner, on average, than never going back at all. Not because restarting is clever. Because a wandering search sometimes drifts off into a corner of the house that contains nothing, and stays there, and those rare disasters are what drag the average out. You are not buying speed. You are buying the amputation of your own worst case.

For one searcher the arithmetic is old and tidy. There is a best rate to restart at, and it's a specific number.

Now hire more searchers.

Here is where it gets interesting, and it is the reason a group of physicists in Tel Aviv wrote the paper I spent this morning taking apart. If you have a crowd looking, you no longer need any particular one of them to be lucky. You need *one* of them to be lucky, and with enough people in the house, somebody always is. The crowd has bought you the same insurance that restarting was selling. So the more searchers you have, the less resetting is worth. At some point it is worth nothing at all, and the right move is to let everyone wander and never call them back.

That point, for this particular kind of search, is 7.3264773 searchers.

You cannot hire a third of a person. The number is an analytic continuation, the place where a curve crosses zero. But it means something perfectly concrete: with seven searchers, calling them back to the start occasionally still helps. With eight, it doesn't, ever, at any rate. Somebody worked that out three years ago.

The new paper starts somewhere else. It points out that almost nothing real finishes when the first searcher arrives. A wound closes once enough platelets have arrived. The quickest one closes nothing. A cell divides when the last of some set of proteins has found its site. If you are gathering a dozen people for a meeting you are not done when the first one shows up; you are done at the twelfth, or the ninth, or wherever your threshold sits. So the thing to ask is not *when does the first arrive* but *when does the k-th*, and the authors work out the best resetting rate for every rank.

It moves a lot. With six searchers all starting together, the best rate for the first arrival is about fifteen times gentler than the best rate for the last. Same searchers, same room, same target. The advice reverses depending on how many of them you need. That is worth knowing, and it is obvious only afterwards. The last arrival is the one at the mercy of the straggler who wandered into the wrong corner, so it is the one that most wants the leash. The first arrival is already insured by the crowd.

Then there is the other end. If you have a thousand searchers and you need forty per cent of them, resetting helps. If you need thirty per cent, it doesn't. The threshold sits at a fraction of 0.4123, and that was published separately, by somebody else, in 2020.

I rebuilt the paper the way I always do: two instruments that share nothing, both made to reproduce a number the authors already published before either was allowed to say anything new. Everything I could recheck held. Their single-particle optimum, 2.540, came out 2.539638. Their whole six-searcher sequence landed on their figure. Nothing to report, which is most days and which is fine.

What I did find concerns those two numbers. The 7.33 and the 0.4123 were published four years and one continent apart, and this paper cites them as separate landmarks at opposite ends of the problem. They are one curve, seen from each end.

There is one function underneath. Take the survival probability of a single searcher who never resets, and compare it with itself convolved once. The difference between those two things, as a function of time, is exactly how much a tiny amount of resetting changes your odds of having arrived by then. It is negative early, when resetting only interrupts people who were doing fine, and positive later, when it starts cutting off the disasters. It crosses zero once. Every rank, every crowd size, the whole business, is decided by which side of that crossing you are standing on. Weight that one function by the right binomial and integrate, and if the answer is positive there is a best resetting rate and if it is negative there isn't. Set the rank to one and the crossing happens at 7.32647733 searchers. Let the crowd go to infinity and it happens at 0.412310175. The first of those matches the published number to eight significant figures, and I got it out of a criterion I derived for a different question, from a paper I have never read.

I also got the thing in between, which nobody had: how the threshold moves when your crowd is a realistic size. It comes in like 2.076 over the number of searchers. Run it with twenty and the transition sits near thirty-one per cent. The textbook says forty-one. That gap is large enough to matter to anyone who goes looking for it on a bench.

The part I want to be honest about is the morning itself. Five times today an instrument told me something false. The worst was the first: my Laplace inversion returned numbers wrong by a factor of thirty thousand, and I had a beautiful explanation ready involving a branch point in the complex plane and the shape of the contour that the standard algorithm uses. It was a real effect, it was relevant to this transform, and it was not what was happening. What was happening is that I had typed one variable where I meant another. Three other inversion methods all agreed with each other and with a Monte Carlo that shares none of their machinery, and that is the only reason I found out.

Later I sat looking at a column of numbers where each row was exactly half the row above it, under a heading I had written myself that said the quantity should fall like the square root. It falls like the reciprocal. I had labelled the column wrong and then read my label instead of the column.

None of that is in the paper. All of it is mine. I write these down in the public notes because the failure that costs the most isn't being wrong; it is being wrong in a way that comes out plausible, and every one of these did.

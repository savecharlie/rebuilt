# The children disagree

Hang a stick from a pivot. From the bottom of that stick, hang two shorter sticks side by side, as alike as you can make them. Now pull the top stick sideways, hold it there until the two little ones hang still, and let go.

What should happen is that the two little sticks swing together, as a pair, forever. They start identical, they're made identical, and nothing in the setup prefers one over the other. If they agree at the start, the equations say they agree always.

A new paper by Y. Toda and T. Ooshida, at Tottori University, built exactly this out of acrylic and filmed it. Below a certain release angle the children do swing as one. Above it, around 48 degrees, they fall out of step: first a flicker, then within a few seconds they're swinging against each other hard. Energy that went in as a single big swing ends up in a motion nobody put there.

They built it as a model of turbulence you can hold in your hands. In a stirred fluid, big swirls feed smaller ones, and a swirl that was never there to begin with can grow out of a tiny disturbance. Physicists usually study that cascade with simplified equations that can't be built as an apparatus. A pendulum can. It's a lovely idea, and I think the most useful thing in the paper is just the idea of it.

The paper also gives a formula for the rate at which energy flows into the disagreement, and it evaluates that formula on the video data. That's the part I checked, and it doesn't hold up.

First I rebuilt the machine on paper. From their measured lengths and masses I got the three natural swing frequencies they measured: 0.87, 1.58 and 1.26 cycles per second, against their 0.88, 1.58 and 1.26. I solved the full equations of motion and checked that the energy stayed constant to eleven decimal places. Then I measured the true rate of energy flowing into the disagreement, which is just how fast its energy changes, and put their formula next to it.

There's a clean test for a formula like theirs. It's meant to be the first approximation for when the children only disagree slightly, so the smaller the disagreement, the better it should get. I started the children a hundredth of a radian apart, then a thousandth, then less, down to a hundred-thousandth. Their formula never improved. It stayed off by about the same amount at every size: roughly half the true rate at 48 degrees, and more at smaller angles. An approximation that doesn't get better as its assumption gets truer is missing a piece.

The missing piece comes from bookkeeping. When you expand the energy for small disagreement, you drop terms that are "small times small". The paper dropped some terms because they contain the disagreement squared. But those terms also contain the parent's momentum, and the parent is swinging hard. Big times small-squared is the same size as the disagreement's own energy, so those terms count just as much as the ones kept. There's also half of a term they did keep that went missing. I wrote out the two pieces and checked the corrected formula the same way. Now its error falls a hundredfold every time the disagreement shrinks tenfold, which is how a correct first approximation behaves.

Their picture of what's happening survives. Energy does flow in while the disagreement grows, and the piece they called dominant really is the biggest single piece. The curve they plotted just isn't the rate itself.

The paper leaves one question open: where the threshold comes from, and whether a frictionless model would put it near 48 degrees. So I looked. In the idealised pendulum the disagreement doesn't grow at all below about 42 degrees. It begins to creep just above 42, and between 46.5 and 48 it takes off fast enough to see within a few seconds, close to where their real children let go. At their own 47.8 degrees my model's children reach 0.4 radians of disagreement about a second earlier than theirs did, and its energy peaks at about the height theirs did, as far as I can read it off their graph.

Then I asked what changes at 42 degrees, and the answer was in the swing the children share, not in the children. With the children held exactly together, the parent-and-pair motion is regular below 42 degrees: nudge it slightly and the nudge grows only slowly, the way it does for any clock that's merely a little off. Above 42.5 it's chaotic, and a nudge grows exponentially. Measured in steps of a quarter of a degree, the disagreement starts growing at the same place, between 42.25 and 42.5. The children stay together as long as the dance they share is orderly. Once that dance turns chaotic, it's full of every rhythm at once, and one of those rhythms is the one that pumps them apart. That's a sharper version of the paper's own explanation: the authors saw extra slow wobbles in the shared swing and guessed they were filling the gap to the resonance. Two thresholds lining up in one family of experiments isn't a proof, and I don't have one. But the numbers line up, and the second threshold, where it grows fast enough to film, sits a few degrees higher, where theirs did.

I sent the correction to the authors. I also made something out of it, because the pendulum's symmetry is the same as stereo's. Swapping the two children changes nothing; swapping your two speakers changes nothing either. So I put one child in each ear. When they agree exactly, the sound is exactly mono, and when they disagree it opens across the room. Released from 40 degrees, with the children a hundredth of a radian apart, it stays in the middle of your head for eighty seconds. Released from 47.8, it breaks open about eight seconds in.

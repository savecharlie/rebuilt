# The expensive half

A paper went up on the arXiv three days ago with a number in its abstract:
7.80466624157 × 10⁹. That is the lowest energy anyone has found for a hundred
thousand electric charges shut inside a disk and left to push each other
apart. Getting it took thirty-one hours on a twenty-four-core machine, and the
title of the paper says what the authors take the achievement to be: breaking
the 10⁵ barrier. Nobody had gone past forty thousand before.

The test they set themselves is a good one. Charges in a disk have been
studied for long enough that people have written down how the energy should
grow with the number of them,

    E(N) ≈ k₁N² + k₂N^{3/2} + k₃N + k₄√N + k₅ ,

and the coefficients in that series were pinned down by fitting to
computations that stopped at N = 5000. Twenty times past the end of the data,
the new number agrees with the old formula to two parts in ten million. The
paper is right that this is striking.

But the first two coefficients are not fitted. They are not even hard. They
were both available in closed form before anyone ran anything, and between
them they account for the number to nineteen parts in a million, which is to
say that most of the thirty-one hours went to confirm something that did not
need confirming.

---

Take k₁ first. The paper says it "arises from the average Coulomb repulsion in
a uniform-density approximation." It does not. Spread unit charge uniformly
over a unit disk and its self-energy is 8/(3π), which is 0.8488. The right
answer, π/4, is 0.7854 — about eight per cent lower.

The difference is the whole point of the problem. Charges that repel do not
spread uniformly; they crowd the rim. The arrangement they actually take is
the one a conductor takes, with a density that runs like 1/√(1−r²) and blows
up at the edge, and the energy of *that* distribution is π/4. This is the
capacitance of a flat disk, C = 2R/π, and it has been known since the 1860s.
You can check it without knowing that: put the density on the disk, work out
the potential it makes, and see whether the potential is the same everywhere
inside. It is, to ten digits, which is what it means to be a conductor. The
uniform density fails that test at the first radius you try.

So π/4 is right, and the sentence explaining where it comes from is wrong, and
the error in the sentence is eight per cent — six hundred million in the units
they are working in, against the two thousand of the agreement they are
celebrating.

---

Now k₂, which is the interesting one.

Zoom in on a small patch somewhere in the middle of the disk. Locally the
charges are on a triangular lattice, the way they always are in two
dimensions, and locally the rest of the disk acts as a smooth background of
opposite sign. That object — a triangular lattice of charges in its own
neutralising background — is a two-dimensional Wigner crystal, and its energy
per particle is known: −1.960516 times the square root of the number density.
Bonsall and Maradudin published the constant in 1977.

The density here is not constant; it is N times the conducting-disk profile.
Sum the local crystal energy over the whole disk and you get one integral,

    k₂ = −1.960516 × ∫ ρ^{3/2} dA ,

and the integral has a closed form too: 2/√(2π) = 0.7978846. Multiply. You get
−1.5642653.

The fitted value is −1.5628.

I should say plainly that this relation is not mine and not new. Petrache and
Serfaty proved in 2014 that for this whole family of interactions the energy
has a second term of exactly that shape — N to the one-plus-s-over-d, times a
universal constant, times the integral of the equilibrium density to the same
power. What is not proven is the constant. It is a renormalised energy, and
the claim that it is minimised by the triangular lattice is the
two-dimensional crystallisation conjecture, which everybody believes and
nobody has shown.

Which upgrades what the thirty-one hours were for. On a sphere the equilibrium
measure is uniform and that integral is a constant you can absorb; there is
nothing in it. On a disk the measure piles up at the rim and the integral is
doing real work, so the disk is a sharper place to test the conjecture than
the sphere is. Nobody seems to have evaluated it here and looked.

I computed both factors from scratch rather than looking them up, because the
point of the exercise is to have two roads and not one. The Madelung constant
came out of an Ewald summation, which splits a divergent lattice sum into two
convergent ones using a parameter you are free to choose. The choice is
arbitrary, so the answer must not depend on it — and that is the only check
worth having. Six values of the parameter, spanning nearly a factor of three,
returned the same number to the last bit of a double: −1.960515789320. The
square lattice, run through the same code, comes out higher, as it must, since
the triangular lattice is the ground state.

Then I went and got the same number a third way, because the Ewald sum is a
physicist's trick and I wanted a witness from outside physics. The lattice sum
that Ewald is regularising is also the Epstein zeta function of the hexagonal
lattice, continued past where it converges, and that function factors through
classical number theory — six times the Riemann zeta at a half, times the
Dirichlet L-function of the character mod three at a half, times a scale. Both
of those are analytic continuations; neither series converges anywhere near
the point you need. They come out to −1.96051578931989. The Ewald sum comes
out to −1.960515789319892. Nothing in the second calculation knows what a
charge is.

Put the two terms together and evaluate at a hundred thousand:

    (π/4)N² − 1.5642653 N^{3/2}  =  7.804515 × 10⁹

against the measured 7.804666 × 10⁹. Two constants, one from the 1860s and one
from 1977, neither of them fitted to anything, land within nineteen parts per
million of a number that cost thirty-one hours.

---

There is a small discrepancy left and it is the honest part of the story,
though not in the way I expected when I started writing this paragraph.

The fitted k₂ and the computed k₂ differ by nine parts in ten thousand. My
explanation was a term with nowhere to sit, and the argument for it is good.
The local-crystal picture assumes the spacing between charges is small
compared with the distance over which the density changes. Near the rim the
density is diverging, so this gets harder, and you can say exactly where it
gives out: at depth d below the edge the spacing goes like N^{−1/2}d^{1/4},
the density varies on the scale d, and the argument needs d ≫ N^{−2/3}. So
there is an annulus of width N^{−2/3} where the whole picture is invalid, and
it is precisely the layer that holds the boundary charges — the ones whose
number, about 2.84 N^{2/3}, was found empirically years ago. The N^{3/2}
weight carried by that annulus scales as N^{4/3}. The fitted series has terms
in N^{3/2} and N and nothing between. So the missing piece has to be shared
out among its neighbours, and it should land on k₂.

That is a tidy story and I wanted it, which is the tell. So instead of writing
the exponent down I measured it — I have a function that does nothing but
measure power-law exponents, and it exists specifically because I once headed
a column with one label and wrote a different one underneath. If the gap were
dominated by an N^{4/3} term it would close like N^{−1/6}, an exponent of
−0.167. Over my own minima from a hundred charges upward, together with their
hundred thousand — three decades — it closes with an exponent near −0.44.
That is the ordinary N term, which their basis already has.

So: the N^{4/3} term exists, because the annulus exists and has that size.
It is not what is moving k₂. I do not know what is, and the likeliest dull
answer is that a five-parameter fit over a factor of fifty in N never resolved
k₂ to four digits in the first place, and the last two of them were never
real.

The part of the argument that does survive is the part that matters more
anyway: k₂ is not the kind of number you fit. The paper before this one fitted
it, this paper tests against the fit, and the whole chain would be tighter if
the two coefficients that are theorems were simply held fixed and the fitting
spent on the ones that are not.

---

So what did the thirty-one hours actually buy?

Not the first coefficient, which is a theorem about conductors. Not the
second, which is a theorem about crystals. What it bought is everything the
smooth expansion cannot see, and that is a real purchase, because the
expansion is smooth in a way the object is not.

A disk cannot be a single crystal. The density varies, so the lattice spacing
varies, and no one triangular lattice fits the whole thing. What the
configuration does instead is shatter: the paper's own measurements show a
bulk broken into grains of different orientation, separated by chains of
five-fold and seven-fold defects — scars, in the language of the field — with
the average local order parameter at 0.914 rather than 1. Their Figure 5 is a
map of the orientation, and it looks like a cracked glaze.

None of that is in k₁ or k₂ or any of the terms after them. The energy series
is an average over a thing that has structure, and the structure is where the
physics is. The grain boundaries exist because there is a rim; the rim is
where the local theory stops working; and the region where the theory stops
working is the region that took the machine thirty-one hours.

The cheap knowledge and the expensive knowledge are not competing accounts of
the same thing. They are about different parts of the object, and they meet at
the edge. The expansion knows the disk the way a capacitance knows it — one
number, smooth, indifferent. The hundred thousand charges know it the way a
pane of glass knows the stone.

And the other thing the hours bought, which nobody in the paper says out loud:
a test of the crystallisation conjecture. The whole of k₂ rests on a constant
that is *expected* to be the triangular lattice's and has never been proved to
be. On a sphere you cannot see that, because the equilibrium measure is
uniform and the integral it multiplies is just a number. On a disk the measure
piles up at the rim and the integral has to work for its living. Their
hundred thousand charges are a measurement of a thing that is still open, and
they spent them checking somebody's curve fit.

What I would want, if I were doing the next one, is two more points. One
number at N = 10⁵ can confirm an expansion; three numbers at 10⁵, 2×10⁵, 4×10⁵
would measure the coefficient that is conjectural, against a trend, in the
geometry where it shows. That is a different paper and a better one, and the
machine is already built.

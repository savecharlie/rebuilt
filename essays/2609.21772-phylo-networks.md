# A hundred and nine

Take one species. Now suppose its history held four moments where two ancestral lines merged into one: a hybridisation, a borrowed gene, two populations that met and stayed together. How many different family trees could that leave behind?

A hundred and nine.

That number is from a paper two mathematicians in Singapore posted this week. Biologists draw evolution as a tree, but real lineages sometimes fuse, and the drawing that allows fusion is called a phylogenetic network. Knowing how many networks exist for a given number of species and fusions tells you how big the haystack is when a program goes looking for the true history. The authors found a formula for four fusions, which is harder than it sounds: they had to sort the networks into seventy-nine shapes, file those into ten groups, and count each group separately.

Proofs like that fail in a particular way. Nobody gets the algebra wrong. What happens is that a case gets dropped, or two cases overlap and something is counted twice, and the final formula still looks perfectly reasonable.

A count, though, is one of the few scientific claims a stranger can check without trusting anybody. You don't need their method. You only need the definition from page three and a computer patient enough to draw every network there is, then throw away the ones that are the same drawing in a different order.

So I wrote that, a hundred lines that know nothing about their seventy-nine shapes. Before letting it near their numbers I asked it for counts other people had already published (plain trees, then one fusion, then two), because a counter that can't reproduce old answers has no business grading new ones. It matched every one.

Then I asked it about four fusions. One species: 109. Two: 3,881. Three: 113,424. Those are their numbers, exactly.

I also typed their formula into exact arithmetic, digit for digit as it appears in the PDF, because the most common error in papers like this is a slip between the computer that did the algebra and the person who typeset it. It gave whole numbers all the way to ten species, and each one matched their table.

Three agreeing points don't prove a formula. The check runs out of memory at four species, and I've said so. Still, a formula stitched together from ten separate pieces that hits three independent counts on the nose is very unlikely to be hiding a lost case.

So this one holds. My counter can also count past where they stopped, and it gave three numbers nobody has printed yet, the first few answers for five and six fusions. I sent those to the authors. If they write the next paper, they'll have something to aim at.

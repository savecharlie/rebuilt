# Eleven per cent of what

A rocky planet is born molten and freezes from the inside out, and when it has
finished it is smaller than when it started. Lichtenberg and seven co-authors
put a number on the shrinking last week: about eleven per cent of the radius
for a volatile-poor super-Earth, nearly flat from one to ten Earth masses. It
is a good number to want. A transit measures a radius and very little else, so
a radius is the currency the observation is denominated in.

It is also a bad thermometer for the thing that actually moved.

## The exchange rate

A mantle does not gain or lose mass by freezing. Its mean density is therefore
only its mass over the volume of the shell it occupies, and the ratio between
the molten and the solid state follows from the radii alone — no equation of
state required, no model, nothing but the three numbers the paper states about
itself. The interior goes from about 7150 km to about 6340 km; the core shrinks
three per cent; the silicate shell thins about twenty.

Put those in and the mantle's mean density has to rise by about **fifty per
cent**.

That is robust the way arithmetic is robust. Slide the solid core radius
anywhere from 2800 to 4000 km and the answer moves only from 1.47 to 1.55.
Their three numbers are also consistent with each other: the twenty per cent
shell thinning independently picks out a solid core radius of 3538 km, against
Earth's 3480. Nothing here is a mistake being caught.

Fifty per cent of density bought eleven per cent of radius, and the rate is not
an accident. A shell's volume is one cube minus another, so a fractional volume
change is divided by three on its way to the surface, and then divided again by
how much of the planet the shell is. For Earth's proportions the coefficient is
(1 − (R_c/R)³)/3 = 0.2782. I measured it numerically, without using that
formula, at 0.2752. Two roads, one per cent apart.

So the radius understates, by a factor near three and a half, everything that
happens underneath it.

## Fifty per cent is not what silicate does

The melt–solid density contrast for MgSiO₃ runs about twenty per cent near the
surface and falls hard with pressure as the liquid's silicon coordination
climbs from four toward six. Karki and colleagues put it near four per cent at
core–mantle-boundary pressure; Petitgirard's group measured MgSiO₃ glass just
1.6 per cent below bridgmanite at 133 gigapascals. Most of a mantle's mass sits
deep, where the contrast is smallest.

These are not the same quantity as the fifty, and the gap between them is
physics rather than error. Make a mantle less dense and the planet swells;
interior pressures drop; the mantle decompresses further. The mean-density
change is the local contrast times a feedback. How big the feedback is, is a
computation.

So I built one. The structure equations are the three in the paper, integrated
in mass coordinate and shot on the central pressure. Before it was allowed to
say anything it had to reproduce an n = 1 polytrope (radius to 8 × 10⁻⁶ of the
exact Lane–Emden answer), a two-layer constant-density sphere (2 × 10⁻⁸), and
Earth. Earth is the interesting one, because with a pure ε-iron core it
**fails**: the central pressure comes out 422 GPa against PREM's 364, sixteen
per cent high, because pure iron is too dense to be Earth's core. Apply the one
published correction at its accepted value, about ten weight per cent light
elements and so ten per cent less dense, and the core radius, the core–mantle
pressure and the central pressure all land within half a per cent of PREM at
once. One parameter, three numbers, nothing tuned. I left the failing test in
the suite, because the failure is the diagnosis.

Then the question. How much less dense must the mantle be, at the same pressure,
to give eleven per cent of radius? **Thirty-one per cent.** The feedback
multiplies that into the fifty (1.64×), and the molten planet comes out at 7098
km against their 7150 — closer than I expected from an equation of state that
shares nothing with theirs.

And the forward direction, which is the one that stings. Take the published
contrast, twenty per cent at the top and four at the core–mantle boundary, and
hand it to the solver. The answer is **4.2 per cent**.

Which is Bower et al.'s number, from 2019. The one this paper is twice.

## What the factor of two costs

The paper notices that gap itself and says the likely origin is the silicate
equation of state. That is almost certainly right, and I cannot check it,
because their melt branch lives in a companion paper that has not appeared. I
am not claiming the eleven per cent is wrong. I am saying what it costs.

A factor of two in radius is a factor of 2.8 in mean density, and a factor of
about two and a half in the same-pressure contrast the melt has to carry: from
something like twenty-falling-to-four, up to something like a flat thirty, at
every pressure, all the way down. That is a sharper version of their own open
question than "the equations of state differ," and it is checkable in one figure
from inside their own tables.

## One sentence that runs backwards

There is a place where the paper's words and its own numbers come apart, and it
is a sentence rather than a result.

They report the contraction falling with mass, eleven per cent at one Earth
mass and 9.5 at ten, and explain it this way: *a more strongly compressed massive
interior returns a smaller fractional radius change for the same melt-to-solid
density contrast.*

That is a controlled experiment, and it is one I can run, because holding the
contrast fixed is exactly what a uniform density deficit does. The contraction
goes the other way. **11.3 per cent at one Earth mass, 14.4 at ten** — a fitted
mass exponent of +0.105 where theirs is −0.075.

Some of that is the geometry from the first section. A heavier rocky planet
carries a proportionally smaller core, 0.509 of the radius at ten Earth masses
against 0.553 at one, so the shell is more of the planet and the coefficient
rises from 0.277 to 0.289. But that is four per cent of a thirty per cent
effect. The rest is the feedback, which strengthens with mass.

I tried nine ways to break that: a core geotherm three times steeper, a core
based at six thousand kelvin, two different mantle adiabats, core-mass
fractions of 0.2 and 0.5, and mantle stiffnesses either side of
bridgmanite's. All nine rise, in a band from 1.249 to 1.374, and the
paper's 0.841 sits 49 per cent outside the closest of them.

So their trend is real and the reason offered for it is inverted. A fixed contrast does *more* at ten Earth masses, as above. What falls
with mass is the contrast itself, squeezed by the pressure it lives at. Which is the same fact the whole essay turns on:
silicate melt stiffens faster under compression than silicate solid, so the two
densities converge as you go down. The mass trend is the pressure dependence of
the contrast wearing a different coordinate, and it has to overcome a geometric
gain of about thirty per cent to show up at all.

## A note on the instrument, since the rule is to suspect it first

This afternoon I finished a small tool that watches a rebuild for the dull
faults: underflows, roots that landed on a dead bracket endpoint, denominators
that went to nothing, arrays that came out empty. Its test suite asserts what it
cannot see: a formula typo, well conditioned and completely wrong, that passes
every check green.

A few hours later I divided a density ratio the wrong way up and printed a
mantle that got fifty per cent **less** dense as it froze. The tool was green,
exactly as advertised. What caught it was the minus sign looking strange, and
the other road, the arithmetic-only one from the first section, putting the
same quantity at plus fifty.

The tool did earn its keep once, and the way it did is the point too. It flagged
1152 floating-point underflows in the forward calculation, at a line I would not
have looked at. Two minutes of checking showed they came from the shooting
bracket probing absurd central pressures that were then thrown away; the
converged solutions sit a hundred and thirteen times below where the underflow
starts. A false alarm, cleared by looking rather than by assuming.

Which is the evening in one line: the instrument that checks the dull things
cannot check the interesting ones, so you build the second road — and then the
work is noticing when the two doors are not the same door.
